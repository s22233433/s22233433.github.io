Lots of comments—does that mean the content works? Reading the different signals in engagement

From giveaway instructions and generic praise to product questions and usage experiences, practice reading comments in context instead of treating engagement volume as direct purchase intent.

ZhenguoCool Editorial · 2026.09.05

In this storyStart with what people are responding toPut comments back in their original contextLeave a question that can be verified

Imagine two videos each receive one hundred comments. One is filled with repeated “Followed and shared,” while on the other people ask about sweetness and storage, and describe their own experience. The comment counts are identical, but they tell you different things.

This is a hypothetical scenario, not a ZhenguoCool client result. The point of this note is to break “lots of engagement” into more concrete observations. Comments are neither purchase records nor a direct readout of what an audience feels; they are more like a small window showing which part of the content some people who choose to speak up are reacting to.

01 / Start with what people are responding to

Do not sort every comment into good or bad too quickly. Start with the comment itself and use a category system that is easy to organize; the four types below are neither official platform rules nor a ranking from low to high performance.

A person may first praise the visual and then ask about product specifications; there is no need to force the two comments into mutually exclusive boxes. If a comment complains about delivery, challenges a claim, or discusses a joke unrelated to the product, note it separately. Do not delete it just to make the results look neat.

02 / Put comments back in their original context

“I want to buy it” may express a real need, or it may simply be a riff; “Does this really work?” may be a genuine question, or sarcasm. Pulling out a few words loses the original tone and conversation. When you read, look at the post, the comment thread, the creator’s replies, and whether a giveaway or question prompt shaped the discussion.

Record how you chose the sample, too. Looking only at pinned or top-ranked comments cannot represent the whole discussion; choosing only the sentences you like makes it easier to find the answer you already wanted to believe. If you are doing a quick read, say plainly that you spot-checked the first twenty visible comments instead of presenting it as an audience-wide survey.

If you want to compare two pieces of content, keep the observation window as consistent as possible. A video published moments ago and one that has accumulated discussion for weeks are naturally at different stages. Record the sample date, visible scope, and selection method so you know what you are comparing later.

03 / Leave a question that can be verified

Instead of writing “This post is highly effective,” try: “Sweetness questions appeared multiple times in the sampled comments; did the video leave out the options?” That sentence does not pretend to know the conversion result, but it can help the next piece of content add the missing information. Then check whether a clear answer already exists, or explain it differently in the next post and observe whether the question changes.

A small exercise for today

Choose one public post and read ten comments you can see. For each, note a “summary of the exact wording,” “context,” and “what is still uncertain.” At the end, write only one question worth continuing to investigate.

What matters is not turning comments into a score that looks precise, but slowing your judgment by half a step: know what you saw, and know what still has no evidence. People who leave no comments are still part of the audience; this method simply cannot hear their voices. A handful of comments cannot support claims about what every audience member thinks.

Editorial and material notes

This is a reading exercise proposed by the ZhenguoCool editorial team, not a platform-metric definition or performance study. The categories, comments, and scenarios are original examples; no client data, real accounts, or private conversations were used.
